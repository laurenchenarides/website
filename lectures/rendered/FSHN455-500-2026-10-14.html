What would improve food access?

Economic mechanisms, research evidence, and policy · FSHN 455/500

Lauren Chenarides

October 14, 2026

Turn the readings into testable explanations

Bitler and Haider ask what a food-desert label tells us about the economic problem. Bonanno explains how demand and retail costs jointly determine store availability.

Suppose a city wants to subsidize a supermarket to improve diets.

What would have to be true about households and retailers for that policy to achieve its goal?

Bitler & Haider (2011; assigned PDF dated 2010); Bonanno (2012).

Title and opening: 3 minutes. Take one prediction from each side of the market. The readings provide a framework, not the final empirical answer. Today’s aim is to follow how economists turn that framework into tests, interpret findings, and evaluate a proposed policy. Avoid repeating Monday’s economics introduction. We will compare a supply counterfactual, a randomized incentive, and a model of retail competition.

Households and retailers respond to each other

Households choose

stores and baskets, given money, time, transportation, and preferences.

⇄
Retailers choose

locations and assortments, given costs, expected sales, and competitors.

Observed purchases reflect both sets of decisions. A store’s assortment is partly a response to demand, and demand depends on what stores offer.

Conceptual framework: Bitler & Haider (2011); Bonanno (2012).

2 minutes. Explain the feedback explicitly. Stocking fresh foods can attract shoppers, but weak expected sales and spoilage risk can discourage stocking. Income and transport affect which stores households can use; those choices affect expected retailer revenue. The same pattern of limited assortment could be generated by different combinations of constraints. This is why the conceptual framework comes before a regression.

A neighborhood comparison does not identify the cause

Neighborhoods with fewer supermarkets also differ in income, transportation, and other conditions that affect food purchases.

Retailers choose locations partly because of expected demand. Households also select where to live.

If we observe fewer stores and less nutritious purchases together, which direction does the causal relationship run?

2 minutes. Ask students for an omitted factor that could affect both store entry and purchases. Define the counterfactual as what would have happened to the same outcome without the intervention. Statistical controls can help but do not automatically resolve unmeasured differences. Transition: we need variation that helps distinguish the competing explanations, and we need to state what assumptions make that variation informative.

Would equal shopping options close the gap?

Lower-income households buy less nutritious groceries on average. Suppose they could buy the same products at the same prices available to higher-income households.

How much of the nutritional purchasing gap would disappear?

A. Less than one-quarter   B. About half   C. Most of it

Choose an answer with a partner. Explain what you are assuming about why purchases differ.

Research question adapted from Allcott et al. (2019).

Give pairs 30 seconds, then take a vote and two explanations. Do not reveal the answer until after the research-design slide. Students predicting a large change are placing more weight on products and prices as constraints; students predicting a smaller change should identify other mechanisms rather than assign blame. All three answers are hypotheses at this point. The counterfactual concerns nutritional quality of grocery purchases, not all dimensions of food access.

Follow households when their options change

Allcott and colleagues studied purchases around supermarket openings and household moves, and compared households facing the same local supply.

They then estimated a demand model to ask what purchases would look like under equal products and prices.

The question is whether changing the shopping environment changes what households buy.

Allcott et al. (2019).

Explain why a comparison between rich and poor neighborhoods cannot by itself answer this question. Store entry and moves help examine changes in exposure, while the model constructs an alternative that is not directly observed. These are distinct pieces of evidence; the numerical decomposition on the next slide comes from the model. Transition: compare the result with the class’s predictions.

Equal products and prices close about 9%

Modeled reduction in the nutritional purchasing gap

9% closes   91% remains in the model.

A. Less than one-quarter. Changing local supply alone explains a small share of this gap.

Allcott et al. (2019). Model counterfactual, not a health effect.

The estimate applies to this study’s purchasing measure and modeled counterfactual. It is not the effect of one new store and it does not establish that the remaining gap is freely chosen. Ask students which initial explanation they would now revise. Transition: a small nutritional effect still leaves other possible benefits worth measuring.

What can we infer from the 9% result?

The model asks about the nutritional quality of grocery purchases under equal products and prices.

It does not estimate the benefit of saving travel time, and “demand differences” do not mean that choices are free of constraints.

Would this finding change your view of a supermarket grant aimed at convenience? What about one justified primarily by better diets?

2 minutes. Have students name the estimand in words: the modeled reduction in the purchasing gap under a specified supply counterfactual. Demand incorporates influences the supply counterfactual leaves unchanged; interpreting that residual as individual fault is not warranted. The model and the changes around openings or moves answer related but distinct questions. Neither randomizes a national equal-supply intervention. A policy conclusion must remain attached to the outcome being studied.

What happens when produce earns a rebate?

The Healthy Incentives Pilot randomly assigned 7,500 SNAP households in one Massachusetts county to receive a 30% rebate on eligible fruit and vegetable purchases.

Other SNAP households received usual benefits.

Why does random assignment tell us more than comparing people who choose to use a produce coupon with people who do not?

Olsho, Klerman, Wilde & Bartlett (2016).

Take one response before explaining selection: people who seek a coupon may already be changing their diets. Random assignment makes the offered incentive the systematic difference between groups at the outset. It does not eliminate concerns about survey response, measurement, or applying the result elsewhere. Transition: this study measures consumption, which differs from the purchasing outcome in the preceding study.

The rebate increased consumption

Adults offered the incentive consumed 0.24 more cup-equivalents per day of the targeted fruits and vegetables.

The 95% confidence interval was 0.13 to 0.34 cup-equivalents per day.

Reducing the effective price of produce changed intake in this trial. The effect may differ where travel costs or retailer participation limit use.

Olsho et al. (2016). Adjusted difference from dietary-recall surveys.

Distinguish an average treatment effect from an increase for every participant. The interval expresses statistical uncertainty; it does not cover every uncertainty about implementation or scale. Ask: would the same offer help someone who cannot reach a participating store? That question reconnects affordability and geography rather than treating the two studies as competing policy rankings.

Random assignment helps; measurement still matters

Assignment identifies the effect of being offered the incentive, rather than the effect of choosing to redeem it.

The dietary analysis included 2,009 adults, or 72% of baseline respondents, with at least one follow-up interview.

What would you check about attrition, dietary recall, and retailer participation before applying the estimate elsewhere?

Olsho et al. (2016).

2 minutes. Ask one student about internal validity and another about external validity, defining those terms if needed. Random assignment balances expected baseline differences; differential follow-up could still bias the analyzed comparison. Self-reported intake can contain error. At scale, the offer’s reach and take-up depend on retailer participation and access. Do not claim the study proves a long-term health effect. The offer also adds resources, so the observed effect is not a pure compensated substitution effect.

These estimates answer different questions

Equal products and prices Produce rebate
Evidence Demand-model counterfactual Randomized offer
Outcome Nutritional purchasing gap Targeted produce intake
Result About 9% of gap closes +0.24 cup-equivalents/day
Policy question How much can supply explain? Does this incentive change intake?

We cannot rank these policies by comparing 9% with 0.24.

Allcott et al. (2019); Olsho et al. (2016).

2 minutes. Ask what a fair policy comparison would require: a common outcome, comparable population, program costs, and implementation details. These are different interventions, designs, and units. Their joint lesson is that the mechanism and the outcome determine what evidence we need. The rebate result does not contradict the supply result. Transition: nevertheless, research can inform concrete policy choices without answering every question.

This approach became part of food assistance

USDA’s nutrition incentives developed from the Healthy Incentives Pilot into FINI and then GusNIP.

GusNIP supports projects that offer fruit and vegetable incentives to people receiving food assistance at the point of purchase.

The practical change is greater purchasing power for eligible foods at participating sellers. A new supermarket is not required for that change.

USDA NIFA: GusNIP, updated July 2026.

This is a concrete connection between an economic mechanism, its evaluation, and an implemented policy approach. FINI is the Food Insecurity Nutrition Incentive program; GusNIP is the Gus Schumacher Nutrition Incentive Program. USDA documents the program lineage; do not claim that one paper alone caused the legislation or that every GusNIP project replicates HIP. Eligible products, incentive rules, retailer participation, and use determine what households actually gain. Implementation creates an opportunity; evaluation establishes whether and for whom it improves outcomes. Transition: retailers matter here too, which brings us back to how markets respond.

Which stores compete most directly with a dollar store?

A dollar store opens in a metropolitan neighborhood. Some shoppers move part of their spending to the new store.

Which existing retailers would you expect to face more pressure?

A. Supermarkets and supercenters
B. Other dollar stores, convenience stores, and small grocers
C. Similar pressure across both groups

Which shopping trips are likely to shift, and which are likely to stay where they are?

Take a one-minute vote and ask for a reason. Wait until the findings slide to return to the predictions. A dollar store could replace a quick trip for a few items but leave a household’s larger supermarket trip in place; ask students to identify when that story would or would not apply. Retail formats are imperfect substitutes, and the overlap is an empirical question. A new option today can also change which options remain after rivals respond. Transition: our research examines whether the competitive effects differ across formats.

A new retailer changes its rivals’ decisions

In our dollar-store study, we linked a spatial demand model to a dynamic model of retailer entry.

We used the estimated model to simulate expansion and competitors’ responses, rather than holding the rest of the market fixed.

Why might the effect in the first year differ from the effect after competing firms respond?

Chenarides, Çakır & Richards (2024).

2 minutes. Explain structural modeling as specifying decisions and constraints, estimating their parameters, and solving the model under alternative conditions. It allows us to ask about market responses to situations not directly observed, but those answers depend on the behavioral model and its fit. Here the relevant outcome is retailer revenue and location decisions, not individual diet. Transition: format-specific substitution produces different effects across competitors.

In our study, small formats faced more pressure

In our simulations of Dollar General expansion, net revenues fell for rival dollar stores, convenience stores, and superettes. Net revenues and store density rose for supermarkets and supercenters.

The study uses Texas retail data from 2014–2019; the competitive analysis focuses on metropolitan markets.

These results do not establish what happens to every rural grocer, or measure the effect on household diets.

Chenarides, Çakır & Richards (2024).

Use we for the coauthored findings. This is a dynamic entry model, not a randomized opening. Exit is not explicitly modeled; the simulations indicate likely exit when a rival’s revenue cannot cover costs. The implication for the policy discussion is to examine which alternatives households might gain or lose. Do not turn a finding about competitors’ revenue and entry into a measured household nutrition effect.

Evaluate the options the household will actually have

An entry restriction preserves the current set of stores only if those stores remain viable. It does not guarantee that a supermarket will replace the blocked entrant.

Allowing entry may improve convenience today while changing the viability of other nearby sellers.

A policy comparison needs plausible retail responses, household travel costs, prices, and assortment—not just a count of openings.

1 minute. These are implications of the framework, not estimated nutrition effects. Ask which households could be harmed if a small-format alternative disappeared, and which could benefit from another low-cost or convenient option. Protecting an incumbent and improving consumer welfare are distinct objectives; they can coincide, but that needs an argument.

Design a pilot that could change your mind

Hypothetical: residents report costly shopping trips and difficulty affording produce. A city is considering a retail grant, a produce rebate, or transport support.

Choose one approach and specify:

  1. The constraint it changes and the primary outcome.
  2. A credible comparison group or rollout design.
  3. A retailer response or implementation problem that could weaken it.

Name a finding that would make you stop, revise, or expand the pilot.

5 minutes. For about 18 students, form six groups of three, two per intervention. Allow two minutes to design, then hear three 45-second reports and a short response. Ask graduate students to articulate an identifying assumption. Accept different designs: randomized rebate offers, randomized transport eligibility where feasible, or a carefully justified comparison for a grant. Do not call any unmatched before-after comparison causal. Use the appendix if someone proposes difference-in-differences and wants to work through it.

State a recommendation at the level of the evidence

“We expect [intervention] to change [outcome] for [population], because it changes [constraint].

Our comparison is [alternative]. The conclusion depends on [assumption], and we will measure [possible unintended effect].”

Which part can the existing research support, and which part requires evidence from this setting?

1 minute. This is the learning check. Listen for an explicit mechanism, a well-defined outcome, and an honest distinction between a published estimate and an extrapolation. Ask for one completed sentence rather than another round of full reports. Begin Q&A by minute 40.

What would change your recommendation?

If a policy adds a store but changes few purchases, was it worth funding?

When would you subsidize a household’s purchases rather than a retailer’s costs?

What evidence would justify restricting entry by a new competitor?

Leave this slide on screen for students’ prepared questions and open discussion. AREC: arrive by minute 50 and keep 25 minutes available. FSHN: arrive by minute 40 and keep 10 minutes available. These questions are optional prompts, not a new sequence to lecture through. Ask students to name the outcome, the alternative, and whose experience they are considering.

Appendix: what is the comparison doing?

Invented example: weekly produce purchases around a retail opening.

Before After Change
Opening area 10 13 +3
Comparison area 8 10 +2

Difference-in-differences: (13 − 10) − (10 − 8) = 1 unit.

A causal interpretation requires parallel trends without the opening, no relevant spillovers, and no coincident change that explains the difference.

Optional, not in the 40-minute core. The three-unit increase alone includes changes that might have occurred anyway. The comparison subtracts the common change, but the arithmetic does not validate the design. Several pre-periods, context about other changes, and checks on who remains in the sample would make the argument more credible. Pre-trends cannot prove the identifying assumption. This example is instructor-created and is not an estimate from any cited paper.

Appendix: a grant changes one part of store viability

A retailer enters when expected future operating returns justify the upfront investment.

A capital grant reduces the initial cost. Greater household purchasing power can increase revenue; reliable distribution can reduce recurring costs.

Which intervention changes entry, which changes continued operation, and which could do both?

Framework: Bonanno (2012); Bitler & Haider (2011).

Optional return to the assigned readings. Entry and continued operation are related but distinct decisions. A construction grant may help the first without solving the second. It is possible for public support to be justified by benefits that the retailer cannot capture, or by an equity objective, but those claims need to be made explicitly and evaluated.
